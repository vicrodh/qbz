## [The end of the line](https://open.qobuz.com/track/32519076)

*September 2026*

As many of you already know, I recently shut down the QBZ repository and every distribution channel under my control.

At the time, I deliberately chose not to explain why. Partly because there were conversations happening that I did not want to interfere with, but mostly because I didn't yet have a clear picture myself of what would happen next.

That said, you didn't exactly need to be Sherlock Holmes to figure out that something had happened.

Over the last few days, I've seen that several users contacted Qobuz directly asking what happened, and Qobuz has now provided an official response. Given that, I think it's appropriate for me to finally say something too.

First, and most importantly: **please don't turn this into a campaign against Qobuz.**

Throughout this entire process, the people I've dealt with at Qobuz have been professional, respectful and considerably kinder than they were under any obligation to be. I genuinely appreciate that.

Qobuz was never an arbitrary choice for QBZ. Of course, sound quality was a big part of it, but it wasn't the only reason I chose their platform. There was also an ethical component to that decision.

Qobuz has consistently positioned itself around the idea that music has value, with particular emphasis on artist and rights-holder compensation. Just as importantly to me, they have chosen to keep a meaningful part of music discovery and editorial curation human, rather than handing everything over to algorithms.

That still matters to me, and what happened with QBZ doesn't erase it.

At the same time, QBZ existed because Linux users had no proper client and independent developers had no official open path to build one. I understand that Qobuz operates within obligations and industry rules much bigger than a single company, but those rules also leave very little room for open source.

QBZ was never trying to fight Qobuz. It simply ended up exactly where those [two worlds collide](https://open.qobuz.com/track/386331920).

## What QBZ proved

There is something positive buried underneath all of this. The response from the community made one thing extremely clear:

> **Linux users deserve good desktop software.**

We're long past the stereotype of Linux being nothing more than a bunch of geeks recycling ancient computers in their basements. Linux users today run high-end workstations, gaming rigs, modern laptops and dedicated media and audio systems. We buy expensive hardware, pay for premium services and care about UX, integration and software that actually takes advantage of the machines we're running it on.

I got to see that firsthand while working on QBZ. In logs users shared with me while reporting bugs or troubleshooting issues, I occasionally found myself looking at audio and computer hardware that cost more than my car.

So no, this isn't a community surviving exclusively on ten-year-old ThinkPads and whatever still boots after being rescued from an office closet.

And there are more of us every year.

The migration toward open platforms is real, and this is no longer a market that can simply be dismissed as an irrelevant niche. Companies will eventually have to decide how early they want to acknowledge that.

I sincerely hope Qobuz does.

## What happens to QBZ?

The answer is simple: I complied with Qobuz's requests, including shutting down the repositories and every distribution channel under my control. I chose to follow those requests completely.

Any realistic future for QBZ as a Qobuz client would, in my opinion, require some form of formal access to the platform. But that would also raise a fundamental question about what QBZ is supposed to be. A closed implementation built around confidential APIs, contractual restrictions or code that could no longer be openly developed would be something very different from the project I wanted to build. Releasing a crippled version with the interesting parts removed just so that something bearing the QBZ name can continue to exist doesn't particularly interest me either.

I would rather let QBZ be what it was than keep its name alive as something I no longer believe in. So QBZ ends here — and for once, that's not me being cryptic.

## But it was worth it

QBZ may have had a premature ending, but I don't consider it a failed project. Quite the opposite: it accomplished what I originally wanted it to accomplish.

It demonstrated that Linux software can be functional, polished and pleasant to use without looking like it escaped from a desktop environment circa 2000, when I used RealPlayer to listen to my ultracompressed .ra files. It also showed that Linux users want — and deserve — proper library management, bit-perfect playback, integrations, metadata tools, sensible UX and native applications that treat the platform as a first-class citizen rather than an afterthought.

And perhaps most importantly, it demonstrated something wonderfully stupid: **if one guy with his ADHD hyperfocus pointed aggressively at building a decent music player can implement the features users have been asking streaming services for, then companies with actual engineering teams can absolutely do it too.**

And they should. Not just Qobuz — all of you.

Build good desktop software. Treat Linux as a real platform. Give users the features they're asking for. Make it fast, make it native, make it nice to use and, while I'm making unreasonable demands, **please, no more Electron.**

Whatever happens from here, don't stop expecting good software. QBZ may no longer be around to fill that gap, but the gap itself didn't disappear. If anything, the reaction to its shutdown proved just how real it is.

Thank you to everyone who tested QBZ, reported bugs, requested ridiculous features, submitted code, translated things, made screenshots, packaged builds, argued with me about UI decisions, recommended it to other people or simply used it to listen to music — and, of course, everyone who sponsored the project (you made it easy to hide the agentic bills from my wife, and they weren't small, haha). And yes, even to those who complained about vibe coding [(which, as you can see, can be done well when there's software engineering behind it)](https://qbz.lol/future.gif).

Thank you for trusting a weird little project that started because one Linux user got tired of waiting for somebody else to build the music player he wanted.

It was a hell of a ride.

And now, apparently, we're all back on the bench together, waiting for a Linux client again.
